The Rorabaugh method

ENCN342 — Hydraulics and Applied Hydrology
University of Canterbury

When you pump a well, the water level drops for two quite different reasons. Some of the drop happens out in the aquifer, where water has to flow radially towards the well — that is ordinary Theis drawdown, and it is proportional to $Q$. The rest happens right at the well itself, where water squeezes through the gravel pack and the screen slots and goes turbulent — and that part grows much faster than $Q$.

\[\Delta h_i \;=\; \underbrace{\beta\,Q_i}_{\textstyle \color{#0072B2}{\text{loss in the formation}}} \;+\; \underbrace{\alpha\,Q_i^{\,n}}_{\textstyle \color{#C1541F}{\text{loss at the well}}}\]

A step-rate test pumps the well at several increasing rates so you can separate the two. Doing that is what the Rorabaugh method is for, and the answer it gives you — the sustainable yield — is what might be useful in a consent application.

Where this fits: after Module 2 (Pumping tests), before Tutorial 6. The data below come from a step-rate test on a rural Canterbury irrigation bore. They are not the tutorial's numbers — different bore, different aquifer, different test — so none of the answers here will match your spreadsheet. The method is the part that transfers.
