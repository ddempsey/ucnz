GEOL338 · a hole in stressed rock

Take the rock out of a hole and the rock left behind carries its load. At the wall the hoop stress is two to three times the far-field stress, and it is highest on the sides facing the smaller stress1.

Notes
  1. Kirsch (1898) solution for a circular hole in an elastic plate, vertical well, θ measured from the SHmax direction. At the wall, hoop stress is S_H + S_h − 2(S_H − S_h)cos2θ − P_w: it peaks at 3S_H − S_h − P_w on the Shmin sides and drops to 3S_h − S_H − P_w on the SHmax sides. Pore pressure is set to zero on this tab. Compression is positive (warm colours); tension is negative (cool colours).
  2. A stressed transparent solid between crossed polarisers shows coloured bands wherever the maximum shear stress passes a multiple of a fixed value. Fringe density is stress concentration made visible. In class: a drilled acrylic sheet squeezed in front of a laptop screen, viewed through polarised sunglasses. Here the max-shear view reproduces that colour cycle (one fringe per 6 MPa); for hoop and radial stress the fringe toggle instead draws 10 MPa contour bands so the sign is still readable against the colour bar.
  3. Mud pressure pushes on the wall (radial compression) but pulls the hoop stress down by the same amount. Push hard enough and the hoop stress goes negative: tension.
  4. The perturbation decays as 1/r². Three radii out the disturbance is about a tenth of its wall value; by four radii it is a few percent. A 20 cm hole disturbs less than half a metre of rock.

Where the hoop stress beats the rock strength, the wall shears and spalls. The two chips sit on the Shmin sides, so a breakout is a compass needle for stress1.

Notes
  1. Mohr–Coulomb at the wall: failure where effective hoop stress ≥ UCS + q·(Pw − Pp), with q = (1+sinφ)/(1−sinφ). The Mohr circle inset uses σ1 = hoop stress on the Shmin side and σ3 = radial stress = overbalance Pw − Pp. Lower the mud and σ3 shrinks while σ1 grows: the circle swells into the envelope. Breakout width: w = 180° − arccos[(S_H + S_h − 2P_p − UCS − (1+q)(P_w − P_p)) / (2(S_H − S_h))].
  2. Rule of thumb from drilling practice: breakouts up to about 60° wide rarely threaten the hole; beyond 120° cavings pile up, the string sticks and the section can collapse. Wider breakouts also mean a larger SHmax, which is how breakout width constrains stress magnitude.
  3. The strip is an unwrapped image of the wall, azimuth left to right from north. Breakouts appear as two broad, blurry dark bands 180° apart, centred on the Shmin azimuth. SHmax is 90° from them. The default 068° is the Taranaki Basin mean from 129 wells (Rajabi et al. 2016).

Push the mud hard enough, or cool the rock enough, and the hoop stress on the SHmax sides turns negative. The wall cracks in tension, in two thin lines 180° apart1.

The plane's depth around the wall is z(ψ) = z₀ + (D/2)·tan(dip)·cos(ψ − dip direction), so the deepest point (the trough) sits at the dip direction and the peak is 180° from it. Breakouts and tensile fractures run parallel to the axis instead, which is how you tell them from natural fractures.
  2. Peak-to-trough height = D·tan(dip), for a hole of diameter D = 216 mm (8½ inch). A 60° fracture in this hole spans 0.37 m of depth; steep fractures make tall sinusoids, flat ones make near-horizontal lines.
  3. Lower-hemisphere, equal-area (Schmidt) projection. A plane plots as a great circle and its pole sits 90° away, on the opposite side of the net. A horizontal plane is the primitive circle with its pole at the centre; a vertical plane is a diameter with its pole on the primitive. The sinusoid trough, the dip direction and the great circle's outermost point all sit at the same azimuth. Drag your pole to set the plane.

Pressure and stress both grow with depth, but at different rates. On a pressure–depth plot a heavier fluid draws a flatter line1, and the mud has to live between pore pressure and Shmin2.

Notes
  1. Gradient = density × g. Water gives 9.8 MPa/km, a 1.2 SG mud 11.8 MPa/km, rock of 2300 kg/m³ 22.6 MPa/km. With depth drawn downward, a larger gradient is a line closer to horizontal. Defaults match Espinoza Example 6.1 converted to SI: at 2134 m, Pp = 21.2 MPa (0.44 psi/ft = 9.95 MPa/km), Shmin = 29.6 MPa, Sv ≈ 48 MPa.
  2. Below pore pressure the well kicks. Above Shmin any fracture that already exists, natural or drilling-induced, is held open and propagated, and the mud is lost: this is the ceiling drillers use, and it is what a leak-off test measures. Breaking an intact wall is a different pressure: the hoop stress on the SHmax side must go tensile, Pb = 3Shmin − SHmax − Pp + Ts (dashed amber), which sits above or below Shmin depending on the stress anisotropy. Here SHmax = Pp + 0.82(Sv − Pp) and Ts = 5.5 MPa from Example 6.1, which puts Pb almost on top of Shmin. Rock strength (breakouts) is left out of this tab and appears on tab 6.
  3. Below the overpressure top, pore pressure is taken to rise at the lithostatic gradient: the fluid is sealed in and carries the weight of the rock being added above it. Because Sv − Pp then stops growing, Shmin tracks the pore pressure and steepens with it.
  4. Shmin is set at the frictional limit for normal faulting: (Sv − Pp)/(Shmin − Pp) = q = (√(μ²+1) + μ)². Stronger faults (higher μ) can hold a larger stress difference, so Shmin drops toward Pp and the window narrows. Example 6.1's stresses sit at this limit for μ = 0.6.
